Environmental analytics

Understand the major factors affecting plantation survival and soil conditions in the surveyed areas of Nashik.

Primary causes of sapling failure

Distribution of causes reported in the community survey (n = 100 responses)

Lack of watering45%
Poor soil quality25%
Stray animals20%
No ownership / follow-up10%

Values are the percentage share of reported causes. Water availability alone accounts for nearly half of all reported sapling losses.

💧 45%

Water availability

Lack of consistent watering is the largest reported factor associated with sapling failure in the current sample — the single most important follow-up gap.

⛰ 25%

Soil quality

Poor soil quality is the second significant factor identified during community environmental observations, linking directly to erosion and topsoil loss.

🐾 20%

External factors

Reports also identified stray animals as a factor affecting newly planted saplings — a low-cost, easily solved protection problem.

Data interpretation

Key environmental insights

Important observations identified from the current survey dataset.

💧

Water drives survival

With 45% of responses pointing to lack of watering, plantation success in the surveyed area depends far more on a maintenance schedule than on the number of saplings planted.

🌱

Soil needs protecting first

A quarter of responses cite poor soil quality. Because eroded topsoil loses nutrients and water-holding capacity, protecting existing soil is cheaper than replacing it.

🤝

Ownership matters

The 10% “no ownership” share shows that a named caretaker per patch is a simple, no-cost intervention that keeps plantation efforts alive after the planting drive ends.

Community survey

100 responses were collected from people in and around the field areas, recording which factors they believe cause newly planted saplings to fail.

📷

Field documentation

Each visit was recorded with photographs, a date, a location description and — where available — GPS coordinates, producing the 18 photographs shown above.

📐

Limitations

The survey is a small community sample, not a laboratory soil test. Percentages describe what respondents reported, and should be read as indicative rather than statistically exhaustive.
